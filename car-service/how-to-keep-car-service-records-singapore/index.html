How to Keep Car Service Records in Singapore

Servicing at the agent feels expensive to me. At an independent workshop I worry about being sold things I may not need, or about something being missed. And when I rely on memory, I forget one thing this time and another the next. So who should keep track of a car's servicing?

By the owner of SG Tyre Hub · Published 26 November 2026 · The views are the owner's; AI helped to organise the text and check sources.

The short answer

Keep the record yourself, in one place, because the car stays with you when the workshop changes. Know three things: what should be done (the maker's service schedule), what was done (job sheets and invoices) and what is still outstanding.

The question many owners ask at the counter

At roadside workshops I have often seen owners walk in and ask the mechanic: “What does my car need this time?”

Now I am facing the same problem myself. Is there really no better way?

Three options, and what each one lacks

The agent's service centre. It usually has a record of the work it did. The cost may be higher, and if some work was done elsewhere, its record may not be complete.

A workshop you know. A good mechanic has experience and may spot a problem by looking or listening. But a mechanic handles many cars. It is not realistic to expect them to remember what was changed on yours last time, or which item was left “for next time”. Even a mechanic may not remember everything about their own car after a while.

Remembering it yourself. A record that travels with the car is not lost when you change workshops. The trouble is that many of us keep it in our heads, or leave receipts in different places and cannot find them when needed. Even when we do, we may not know what comes next.

So the problem is not only about which workshop you use. Often nobody is responsible for keeping one complete record of the car.

My view: the owner should hold the record

Workshops can change and mechanics can move on, but the owner stays with the car. You do not need to know how to repair it. You only need to separate three things:

  1. What should be done: based on the maker's service schedule.
  2. What was actually done: based on job sheets and invoices.
  3. What is still outstanding: items checked but not done, and items advised for next time.

A simple method in four steps

Step 1: Find your car's service schedule. Start with the owner's manual and service booklet. If you cannot find them, ask the dealer who sold you the car or the brand's agent. Some cars also show service prompts on the dashboard. Requirements can differ by model, year and use, so do not simply copy another car's list. If you drive a Honda Civic, see the Civic service packages in Singapore.

Step 2: Write one entry for every visit. A phone note, a spreadsheet or a small notebook all work. What matters is keeping it in one place.

It is fine if older records are incomplete. Start with this visit. If you cannot recall something, write “not sure” instead of guessing.

Step 3: Show the record before work starts. Show the mechanic your last entry and the items in the manual for this stage. For each extra item suggested, ask politely:

“What is this based on? Is it due under the service schedule, did the inspection find a problem (may I see it), or is it advice to do it early?”

All three can be good reasons. Once you know which it is, you can decide whether to do it now or later. For safety items such as brakes, tyres and steering, do not put them off just to save money. If you are unsure, get a second inspection.

Step 4: Record it before you leave. Collect the documents and write the entry on the spot. After a few days it is easy to miss things.

Is there an app for car service records?

Yes, quite a few, which suggests I am not the only one who forgets. We came across the ones below while reading up. We have not tried or tested any of them. The descriptions are based on their own web pages and are not recommendations. Features and fees may change.

  • General maintenance log apps: for example Loggy and Autozis. CARFAX Car Care also offers records and reminders; its vehicle history data leans towards the United States.
  • Chinese-language apps: there are some on Apple's App Store. We have not confirmed that they can be downloaded in Singapore. Check the developer's name before you download.
  • For technical users: the open-source LubeLogger records maintenance and fuel. You need to host it yourself.
  • With workshops taking part: Digital Servicebook, based overseas, lets workshops add records that owners can view. We did not find out whether it works in Singapore.
  • In Singapore: Motorist's car servicing page says your servicing record stays with your vehicle profile. The page does not say what the record contains, or whether you can add work done elsewhere. Ask before relying on it.

Questions to ask when choosing: Can you record work done at any workshop? Can you store photos of invoices and job sheets? Can you export your records if the app stops working or you change phones? What are the limits of the free plan? What personal data does it collect? Cover up personal details you do not need to share before uploading an invoice.

A tool is only a place to keep the record. Any of them can work, and so can a notebook. What matters is writing it down every time and having it with you at the workshop.

Can I service my car outside the authorised workshop during warranty?

Going elsewhere does not by itself cancel the warranty, but conditions apply. The Competition and Consumer Commission of Singapore (CCCS) worked with major authorised dealers to change their warranty terms in 2017, and issued a reminder in July 2024. In summary:

  • Authorised dealers should not require all servicing and repairs to be done at their workshop, or reject a warranty claim solely because a third-party workshop serviced or repaired the car.
  • They may still require warranty repairs, where the cost is claimed under the warranty, to be done at authorised workshops.
  • They may reject a claim if they can establish that the fault was caused by a third party's repair or servicing, or by the owner's failure to service the car properly and periodically according to the maker's recommended schedule. They may also reject claims for parts that a third party replaced or modified.
  • Where the warranty terms require documents, CCCS advises owners to keep them safe. They may need to show service dates and the specifications of parts used, to show that the maker's recommendations were followed.

Read your own warranty terms before you act, ask the dealer if anything is unclear, and keep your records and invoices. For parallel-imported cars, check the warranty arrangement line by line.

What this method cannot do

A record does not replace an inspection. Someone who knows cars still has to judge how worn a part is. Nor does a record guarantee that every workshop you meet is reliable.

What it can do is stop you from guessing from scratch each time: what was done, what is outstanding and what was advised is all there.

In the end the owner still has to keep it up, and it is easy to give up if it feels like a chore. I am still looking for an easier way myself.

How do you keep track? A notebook, a spreadsheet, your phone, or an app you like?

Common questions

What should a car service record include?

The date, mileage, workshop, each job done, parts and their specifications, cost, items checked but not done, advice for next time, and photos of the job sheet and invoice.

I have no old records. Where do I start?

Start from your next visit. Mark anything you cannot confirm as “not sure” instead of filling it in from memory. Gather any invoices you can still find.

Does an independent workshop void my car warranty?

Not by itself, according to the CCCS releases about authorised dealers. Claims can still be rejected in some situations, so read your own warranty terms and keep your service documents.

Do I need an app?

No. A notebook or spreadsheet works if every visit is recorded in one place and you can show it at the workshop.
