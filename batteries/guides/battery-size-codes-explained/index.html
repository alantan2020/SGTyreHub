Car Battery Codes Explained: 55B24, 80D26, 105D31 and DIN66

The code printed on your battery is not random. It tells you the size, the rough power rating and which side the positive terminal sits on. Here is how to read it.

Car Battery Codes Explained: 55B24, 80D26, 105D31 and DIN66

Japanese-size codes: 55B24L, 80D26L, 105D31R

Most Japanese and Korean cars, and many cars sold in Singapore, use a battery code in the JIS style. A code such as 80D26L has four parts.

The first number (80) is a performance rating. It is a relative figure, not a capacity in amp-hours. In the same case size, a bigger number means more starting power. The letter (D) is a size group for the width and height of the case. The second number (26) is the approximate length of the case in centimetres. The last letter (L or R) tells you which side the positive terminal is on.

Why the last letter matters

A 55B24L and a 55B24R are the same size and rating. The difference is that the terminals are on opposite ends. If you fit the wrong one, the cables may not reach, or they may be stretched across the battery, which is unsafe. When you replace a battery, the positive terminal must be in the same position as on your old battery. Check this on your old battery before you buy.

Two batteries with similar codes are not always interchangeable

A bigger first number in the same case size is usually fine, because the battery is simply stronger. A different size group letter or length number means the case is a different size and may not fit the tray or the hold-down clamp. Terminal height and the vent layout can also differ between brands, so a photo of your old battery helps us confirm the match.

European codes: DIN66, DIN70, LN3 and similar

Many European cars, and some newer cars of other makes, use European-style codes. DIN66, DIN70 and DIN77 describe both the size and the capacity class. LN2, LN3 and LN4 are another European size series. The terminal position is built into these sizes, so you do not choose left or right the way you do with Japanese codes.

European cars are more likely to use AGM or EFB batteries and to need the new battery registered with the car's computer. See our guides on that below.

What to do if you cannot read the code

Batteries get dirty, and labels fade. Wipe the label with a dry cloth, take a photo in good light, and send it to us together with your car model and year. You do not have to understand the code yourself. We will read it and confirm the right replacement with you.

  • Photograph the whole label, not only the code.
  • Show the side with the two terminals, so we can see where the positive (+) terminal is.
  • Note any words such as EFB, AGM or Start-Stop.

Common questions

Does the first number on the battery code mean amp-hours?

No. In Japanese-style codes such as 80D26L, the first number is a performance rating, not a capacity in amp-hours. Compare it only between batteries of the same case size.

Can I use a battery with a higher first number?

Often yes, if the size group letter, the length and the terminal side are the same. The battery is then stronger, not different. Send us your label and we will confirm the match.

What if my battery has no L or R?

European sizes such as DIN66 or LN3 do not use L and R. For other codes, send a photo of the terminals and we will check which side the positive terminal is on.
