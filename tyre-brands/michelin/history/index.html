Michelin Tyres: History, Innovations and Tyre Families — A Singapore Driver's Guide

If you drive in Singapore, there is a good chance the tyres on your car, or on the car next to you at a red light, carry the Michelin name. This guide tells the story behind it: where the company came from, what it actually invented, why it keeps turning up in motorsport and in restaurant guides, and what the main tyre families mean when you are choosing a set for your own car.

1. Before Michelin: rubber comes to Clermont-Ferrand

The story begins with a bouncing ball. In the early 1800s a Scottish woman, Elisabeth Pugh-Barker, married an Auvergne businessman named Édouard Daubrée. Her uncle was the chemist Charles Macintosh, who had worked out that rubber could be dissolved in benzine. Remembering the rubber balls he had made for her as a girl, she began making her own in her husband's workshop. That small habit is how rubber arrived in Clermont-Ferrand.

Daubrée later teamed up with his cousin Aristide Barbier to build farm machinery. Their company, Barbier-Daubrée, won a name at international trade fairs partly because its machines used so many rubber parts: hoses, valves, joints and fittings.

When both founders died in the 1860s, the business was in trouble. Aristide Barbier's daughter Adèle, who had married Jules Michelin, believed rubber still had a future. She asked her sons André and Édouard to step in, and Édouard took charge and renamed the firm Michelin & Cie. The brothers' first own product, in 1889, was a rubber brake pad for horse-drawn cabs and carriages. They called it "The Silent". It was the first rubber object Michelin made and sold, and it already carried the first Michelin logo.

2. 1891: the modern tyre is born

One day a cyclist with a flat tyre wheeled his bicycle into the factory yard. In those days bicycle tyres were glued onto the rim. The method held well, but repairing a puncture meant hours of scraping off glue and waiting for new glue to dry.

Édouard Michelin took the problem personally and designed a detachable tyre. With it, mending a puncture took about fifteen minutes instead of hours. The idea proved itself quickly: the cyclist Charles Terront rode Michelin detachable tyres to win the Paris–Brest–Paris race, finishing more than eight hours ahead of the runner-up. For a young company, that was the best advertisement imaginable.

3. 1895: the first car to run on air

André and Édouard expected the motor car to grow fast, so they applied the same thinking to it. They fitted air-filled tyres to a car called L'Éclair and entered it in the Paris–Bordeaux–Paris race. L'Éclair did not win. But it became the first car in the world to run on air-filled tyres, and the brothers used the stunt to show drivers what pneumatic tyres promised: reliability, durability, safety and comfort.

4. 1898: Bibendum, the Michelin Man

According to Michelin, the Michelin Man first took shape in a stack of tyres. His outline came from the imagination of André and Édouard and was drawn by the artist Marius Rossillon, known as O'Galop. The same year he appeared in a composition titled "Nunc est Bibendum" (Latin for "now is the time to drink") and made his first public appearance at the Michelin stand at the first Paris Motor Show.

He has been working ever since. In 2000 the Financial Times named him "Best Logo of the Century", and in 2018, on his 120th anniversary, he received a Millennium Icon Award at Advertising Week in the United States.

5. 1899: the first car past 100 km/h

In 1899 the Belgian driver Camille Jenatzy wanted a speed record. Michelin equipped his electric car, La Jamais Contente ("Never Satisfied"), with special tyres. They were smaller in diameter and wider across the tread, which made the car steadier, and they already looked like the tyres we know today. With them, Jenatzy's car was the first in the world to pass 100 km/h.

6. 1900 to 1926: the guide, the map and the signpost

Early motoring was an adventure. Garages were rare, roads were poorly marked and a puncture could end a day. André Michelin saw that selling tyres meant helping people travel.

  • 1900, the first Michelin Guide. A small red booklet with tyre-repair advice and practical addresses: garages, fuel stops, tyre shops, hotels.
  • 1910, the road map. A 1:200,000 map to go with the Guide, so drivers could find their way.
  • 1912, road numbering. André campaigned for roads to be numbered. After the First World War he went further and had Michelin produce distance markers and road signs, made from enamelled lava plates on reinforced-concrete bases. Michelin built several thousand of them.
  • 1926, the star. The Guide that introduced the "fine dining star" came out in 1926 and grew into a reference for people who care about food.

So when you see a "Michelin-starred" restaurant, the same company that made your tyres, and not a food publisher, is behind the name. It began as a tool for drivers.

The Guide reached Singapore much later. The first Singapore edition was announced in late 2015 and revealed at a gala dinner at Resorts World Sentosa on 21 July 2016. Michelin Guide director Michael Ellis was reported as saying Singapore was the next Asian city for the Guide after Japan, Hong Kong and Macau, and the first in Southeast Asia (press reports; we have asked Michelin to confirm the details).

7. The First World War: tyres, tents and aeroplanes

When 3,500 Michelin workers were called to the army, the company concentrated on tyres, wheels and essential rubber goods such as sleeping bags, tents and mugs. The brothers believed the war would be decided in the air, so part of the factory went over to building aircraft: from 1915 to 1918 the factory turned out close to 2,000 Bréguet aircraft. So that they could fly out in any conditions, the company laid what it describes as the world's first concrete runway, at Aulnat near Clermont-Ferrand (a 1916 photo caption on Michelin's heritage page dates the track).

8. 1931: the Micheline, a train on tyres

André Michelin, kept awake on a noisy sleeper train, wondered whether train wheels could run on tyres. The company designed a special tyre with a guide ring that could roll along the narrow rail surface and carry real weight. The result was the Micheline, a railcar on tyres. On 10 September 1931 prototype number 5 made its first trip between Paris and Deauville.

9. Citroën and the 2CV

Michelin gained control of the French carmaker Citroën, with Pierre Michelin and Pierre Boulanger running it. The pair began the TPV programme, an abbreviation of "Toute Petite Voiture" (very small car). The idea came from one of the first big consumer surveys in motoring, the National Popular Automobile Survey that the Michelin brothers ran in 1922: a car that would be simple and versatile, undemanding to drive and, first of all, cheap to own. It became the Citroën 2CV. Michelin's heritage material also mentions a recent project to bring the 2CV back for the age of electrification, to which Michelin says it contributes its expertise.

10. 1937: rubber meets steel

From around 1930 cars grew heavier and faster, and Michelin engineers rebuilt the tyre's structure. They added metal wires and spent about six years finding a way to make rubber and steel bond permanently. In 1937 the first steel-cased tyre went on sale. It was a quiet but important step: it made the next breakthrough possible.

11. 1946: the radial tyre

The breakthrough came with a prototype nicknamed the "fly cage", because the cables in its casing were spaced so widely apart. Its design was the origin of the radial tyre. Michelin filed the patent on 4 June 1946, and the tyre went on sale as the MICHELIN X.

The X tyre lasted two to four times longer than a conventional tyre, was safer, and used less fuel. Radial technology then spread to every kind of vehicle: heavy trucks (1952), civil-engineering vehicles (1959), farm tractors (1979), aircraft (1981) and motorcycles (1987).

This is the main reason Michelin features in any history of tyres. Almost every car tyre sold today is a radial.

François Michelin, grandson of the founder, became co-manager of the company alongside Robert Puiseux and pushed radial tyres into every car market. Under that strategy Michelin rose from tenth place to become the world's largest tyre maker.

Research and testing

To support the radial roll-out, Michelin put most of its money into developing the X tyre. It opened a research and test centre at Ladoux, near Clermont-Ferrand, in 1965, with laboratories, workshops and test tracks. Further centres came in Spain (Almería, 1973), the United States (Laurens, 1977) and Japan (Ota, 1991). Michelin also built unusual test vehicles. One of them, the Milles Pates ("Thousand Legs", also called the PLR for "Poids Lourd Rapide", or fast heavy truck), was built in 1972 to test truck tyres.

12. Motorsport: racing as a laboratory

Michelin stepped away from racing in 1912, then came back after the X tyre won the 24 Hours of Le Mans unexpectedly in 1951. A string of titles followed:

  • 1973: first World Rally Championship, with Alpine-Renault. The same year Michelin set up a dedicated motorsport department.
  • 1979: Formula 1 World Championship.
  • 1984: MotoGP World Championship.
  • 2018: 400th MotoGP victory.

Michelin describes racing as a testing ground for new ideas, and it is one reason the Pilot Sport family you will read about below carries a sporting name.

13. Becoming a global company

Success with the radial tyre pushed Michelin to expand. Starting in 1975 it opened four US plants, three in South Carolina (Greenville, Anderson, Spartanburg) and one in Alabama (Dothan), and the X tyre did well on other continents too. During the 1980s and 1990s the company reached emerging markets in Asia and Eastern Europe, with new plants in Asia (Japan, Thailand, Korea and China) and in Eastern Europe (Slovakia, Poland, the Czech Republic, Hungary and Russia).

14. The "green tyre" and fuel economy

In the early 1990s, when the environment was hardly a selling point, Michelin introduced the "green tyre". By adding silica to the rubber mix, it reduced rolling resistance, which lowers fuel use and CO2. The idea was shown at the Paris Motor Show in 1992 as the MXN (the N stands for Nature), and in 1994 launched the first range, called MICHELIN Energy.

Rolling resistance still matters today, especially for electric cars, where it affects range.

15. Looking forward

  • Challenge Bibendum (1998–2014). A series of 12 events around the world on clean and sustainable vehicles. In 2017 it became Movin'On, a world summit on sustainable mobility.
  • UPTIS (2019). An airless tyre concept designed so that punctures, and the need for a spare wheel, disappear.
  • VISION concept. A tyre imagined as biosourced, connected and with a renewable tread, covered by 19 patents and used as a guide for Michelin's research teams.
  • Michelin in Motion (2021). Michelin's strategy to 2030, combining tyre growth with connected mobility and high-technology materials. In 2023 it bought Flex Composite Group.

15b. Beyond tyres: maps, travel services and lifestyle

Michelin has never been only a tyre company. Alongside the Guide and maps it built travel-assistance products and services for drivers. In 2000, the year the Michelin Man was named best logo of the century, the group created Michelin Lifestyle Limited, a subsidiary that designs and markets tie-in products for mobility, sport and leisure under licence with partner manufacturers. In the 2010s Michelin also bought the Spanish online restaurant-booking platform Restaurantes.com, which has since been renamed TheFork and operates in more than 20 European countries, according to Michelin's heritage page.

16. Three tyre ideas explained, using Michelin's own guidance

These explanations are based on Michelin Singapore's tyre advice pages. They are useful whatever brand you buy.

Rolling resistance: why tyres affect fuel use and EV range

Rolling resistance is the energy a tyre loses as it rolls: the tyre deforms, it turns through air, and it rubs on the road. The higher it is, the more energy the car needs to keep moving. Material, structure, tread pattern, width, diameter and tyre pressure all change it.

Michelin points to one number drivers can control: tyre pressure. It cites French road studies in which more than half of cars ran at least 0.3 bar below the correct pressure. According to Michelin, 0.3 bar of under-inflation raises rolling resistance by about 6%, and 1 bar by about 30%. (Check your own pressure against the placard on your car, see our guide to tyre pressure in Singapore.)

You cannot remove rolling resistance entirely. The friction between tyre and road is also what gives you grip. The aim of a low-rolling-resistance design, such as the silica-based "green tyre" idea described earlier, is to lose less energy as heat without giving up grip.

Tread depth and wear indicators

Michelin's Singapore site states that 1.6 mm is the minimum tread depth, below which the tyre must be replaced. It also says Michelin designs its tyres to keep performing until they reach that limit, so replacing early wastes money. A small raised block in the grooves, the wear indicator, shows when the limit is reached. On Michelin tyres it is marked with a small Michelin Man. See our guide on when to replace tyres in Singapore for how to check.

Run-flat tyres

A run-flat tyre has reinforced sidewalls that carry the weight of the car after a loss of pressure, so you can keep moving instead of stopping at the roadside. Michelin's run-flat tyres are marked ZP or EMT on the sidewall; other makers use other codes. According to Michelin, you can continue for up to about 80 km at up to about 80 km/h, but the real figure depends on load, temperature and road. Michelin says a run-flat may be repaired just once, by a professional, and never if its sidewall says it cannot be repaired. Whether a run-flat can be repaired is decided by each tyre maker, so ask a professional before assuming it can.

Notes for Singapore drivers

  • Winter and all-season tyres: Michelin's winter families (Alpin) are for snow and ice, which Singapore does not have. Tyres for our wet, hot roads are the summer-type families above.
  • Tyre age matters as much as brand. Every tyre has a four-digit date code on its sidewall. See our guide on how to read it.
  • Check the size first. The right tyre is the one that matches your car's size and load rating, whatever the brand.

18. Frequently asked questions

Is the Michelin Man called Bibendum? Yes. The name comes from the Latin line "Nunc est Bibendum" in an early composition featuring him.

Is Michelin the company behind the Michelin Guide? Yes. André Michelin created the first Guide in 1900 to help motorists, and the "star" edition followed in 1926.

What did Michelin invent? Several things, not all in one go: a detachable bicycle tyre (1891), the first car driven on air-filled tyres (1895), the steel-cased tyre (1937) and the radial tyre (patent filed 1946), plus the rail tyre, the Guide and road signs.

Are all Michelin tyres the same? No. The families above are designed for different needs. Ask us which one fits your car.
