Tyre Rotation, Balancing and Alignment: What Each One Does

These three jobs sound alike and are often confused. They fix different problems, and knowing which one you need saves money.

Tyre Rotation, Balancing and Alignment: What Each One Does

Rotation: moving tyres around the car

Front and rear tyres wear at different rates, and front-wheel-drive cars usually wear the front tyres faster. Rotating them, moving them between positions, evens out the wear so the set lasts longer. Many car makers suggest rotating roughly every 10,000 km, but your manual has the exact interval.

Balancing: fixing a vibration

A tyre and wheel are never perfectly even in weight. Small weights are added to the rim so that the wheel turns smoothly. If it is out of balance, you feel a vibration in the steering wheel or the seat, often at motorway speeds. Balancing is done whenever a tyre is fitted, and again if you feel the vibration.

Alignment: fixing a pull or uneven wear

Alignment sets the angle of the wheels to the road and to each other. If it is out, the car may pull to one side, the steering wheel may sit off centre, and the tyres wear unevenly on one edge. A hard knock against a kerb or a pothole can put it out. Alignment is a separate job from balancing.

Which symptom means which job

A shake that comes and goes with speed points to balancing. A pull to one side, or a steering wheel that is not straight, points to alignment. Wear that is worse on one edge of the tread points to alignment or pressure. Tyres that wear faster at the front than at the back call for rotation.

Common questions

Do new tyres need an alignment?

Not necessarily. It is worth checking if the old tyres wore unevenly, or if the car pulls.

Can I rotate tyres on any car?

Not always. Some cars have different front and rear sizes, or directional tyres. Check the manual, or ask before moving them.

Why is my steering wheel shaking at 90 km/h?

That is a classic sign of a wheel being out of balance, and it is usually quick to correct.
