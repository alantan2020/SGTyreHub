Car Battery Draining Overnight: Causes and How to Find the Drain

If the car starts fine in the evening and is dead in the morning, either the battery can no longer hold a charge or something is drawing power while the car is off. Here is how to tell which, and what to check first.

Car Battery Draining Overnight: Causes and How to Find the Drain

Two different problems

A battery that drains overnight usually falls into one of two groups. In the first, the battery itself is worn out and cannot hold a charge, so even a small normal draw empties it. In the second, something in the car is using too much current while it is parked, which is called a parasitic drain. The fix is different, so it helps to know which one you have.

A battery older than about three to five years, one that struggles on short trips, and one that has been flat more than once are all signs of the first group.

Common causes of a parasitic drain

A modern car is never fully off. The alarm, the central locking and the clock use a very small current, and the electronics go to sleep a few minutes after you lock the car. A problem is when something keeps them awake or draws more power than it should.

Typical culprits are a boot light, glovebox light or vanity mirror light that stays on, a door that does not register as shut, a hard-wired dashcam in parking mode, a phone charger or USB device left in, an aftermarket alarm or audio amplifier, and a faulty module that does not go to sleep. A failing alternator can also drain the battery if its diode lets current flow backward.

Short trips and long gaps

A battery can also run down with no fault at all. In Singapore, many cars do short stop-start trips that never give the alternator long enough to refill the battery after starting. Add a long gap, such as a week of the car sitting in a car park, or a holiday overseas, and the battery slowly loses charge. If that sounds like your car, a longer drive every week helps. For a long trip abroad, see our guide on whether to disconnect the battery before travelling.

How to test for a drain at home

The test needs a multimeter that can read current in amps. Switch the car off, close the doors and wait about 30 minutes for the electronics to go to sleep. Loosen the negative battery clamp, and connect the meter in series between the clamp and the negative terminal, so that the car's current flows through the meter. A healthy modern car typically shows a current in the tens of milliamps once asleep. A reading of hundreds of milliamps, or amps, means something is drawing too much.

To find the circuit, pull the fuses one at a time and watch the reading drop. The circuit that makes the current fall is where the problem is. Take care: do not start the car or open and close doors with the meter connected, and if the car has battery registration or a start-stop system, ask a workshop before disconnecting anything.

When to replace the battery

If the drain test is normal and the battery still goes flat, the battery is the problem. A workshop can test its capacity. Common questions

How long can a car sit before the battery goes flat?

It depends on the battery and the car. A healthy battery in a car with a normal draw can sit for a few weeks, but an older battery, or a car with a dashcam or alarm, may not last a week.

Will jump-starting fix a battery that drains overnight?

It gets you moving, but if the cause is a drain or a worn battery it will go flat again. Find the cause.

Can a dashcam drain the battery?

Yes, if it is hard-wired and records in parking mode. Many have a low-voltage cut-off, which should be set correctly.

Is it the alternator or the battery?

If the battery is flat after the car has been driven for a long trip, suspect the alternator or the battery. A workshop can test charging voltage in a few minutes.
